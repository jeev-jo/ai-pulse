The Cracks Are Showing

Arm built its first chip in 35 years. SK hynix filed for a blockbuster US IPO. Congress moved to ban data centers. David Sacks left the White House. And 76% of Americans said they don't trust AI.

Based on events from March 21 – April 5, 2026
Sources cited throughout
Approx. 10 min read

Last issue, the faith was enormous. Nvidia pitched an $85 trillion future. Bezos raised $100 billion to buy factories. Anduril won a $20 billion Army contract. The money was moving so fast that nobody stopped to check whether the foundation could hold it.

This issue, the foundation started cracking — not from failure, but from pressure.

In the two weeks since Issue #004, the chips, the energy, the politics, and the public trust all moved at once. Arm shipped its first in-house chip in 35 years. SK hynix filed for a US IPO that could raise $14 billion. Congress introduced a bill to ban data center construction. David Sacks stepped down as White House AI czar. And a Quinnipiac poll found that 76% of Americans don't trust AI companies to do the right thing.

The money is still flowing. The trust is not. Here's what happened.

Part OneThe Chip Wars Escalate

On March 24th, Arm Holdings did something it had never done in its 35-year history: it released a chip of its own. The Arm AGI CPU is a production-ready processor built for AI data center inference, developed using Arm's Neoverse family of CPU cores in partnership with Meta, which is also its first customer. [1]

This is a historic break. Arm has spent its entire existence licensing chip designs to others — Apple, Nvidia, Qualcomm. Now it's competing alongside its own customers. The company, majority-owned by SoftBank, also listed OpenAI, Cerebras, and Cloudflare as launch partners. The chip was already in development since 2023 and is ready to order now. [1]

Three days later, SK hynix — the South Korean memory chip giant — confidentially filed a Form F-1 for a US listing that analysts expect could raise $10 billion to $14 billion. SK hynix makes the high-bandwidth memory (HBM) that Nvidia's AI chips depend on, and it has a market cap of around $440 billion. But it trades at a persistent discount to US peers, and the IPO is an explicit attempt to close that gap. [2]

The backdrop: a global memory crisis dubbed "RAMmageddon" that's expected to last until at least 2027. SK hynix is planning $400 billion in semiconductor investments through 2050, including a new cluster in Yongin, South Korea, and a $7.9 billion deal with ASML for extreme ultraviolet lithography scanners. Samsung's investors are now pushing for its own US listing. [2]

"SK hynix's U.S. listing could help close a long-standing valuation gap with global peers. Despite having comparable — or in some areas stronger — production capacity than U.S.-based chipmakers, the Korean company has historically traded at a discount."

Seoul-based semiconductor analyst, via TechCrunch, March 27, 2026

Meanwhile, South Korean fabless chipmaker Rebellions raised $400 million at a $2.3 billion valuation in a pre-IPO round, bringing its total raised to $850 million. [3] And Microsoft launched three new foundational models — MAI-Transcribe-1, MAI-Voice-1, and MAI-Image-2 — positioning itself as a cheaper alternative to Google's and OpenAI's offerings under what CEO Mustafa Suleyman is calling "Humanist AI." [4]

The hardware layer is fracturing. Arm is competing with its own licensees. SK hynix is racing to close a valuation gap with Western peers. Korea is emerging as the second front in the chip war. And Microsoft is quietly building its own model stack. Nobody wants to depend on Nvidia alone — and the moves are accelerating.

Part TwoThe Data Center Backlash

The AI industry's appetite for energy caught up with it in the last two weeks — and the backlash came from every direction at once.

The numbers are staggering. Microsoft, Chevron, and Engine No. 1 are building a 5-gigawatt natural gas power plant in West Texas. Google and Crusoe are constructing a 933-megawatt gas-fired facility in North Texas. Meta is adding seven more gas plants to its Louisiana complex, bringing its total capacity to 7.46 gigawatts — enough to power all of South Dakota. Gas turbine prices have surged 195% and new orders are now pushed out to 2028. [5]

Congress responded. On March 25th, Senator Bernie Sanders and Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez introduced companion legislation to ban all new data centers with peak power loads over 20 megawatts until Congress enacts comprehensive AI regulation. The bill would also require government certification of AI models before release, mandate union labor in data center construction, and prohibit exporting advanced chips to countries without similar rules. [6]

The next day, the Senate demanded that data center operators disclose their power bills — the first concrete oversight move from Congress on AI energy consumption. [7]

"Everyone's fighting over who has the best model. Meanwhile, the communities where these things actually get built are fighting over whether they can keep the lights on."

On a Kentucky farmer rejecting a $26M data center offer, TechCrunch, March 24, 2026

And then there was Teresa Collins. A Kentucky woman who rejected a $26 million offer to turn her farm into a data center. Her refusal became a symbol of the growing tension between the AI industry's need for land and the communities that actually live on it. [8]

The AI boom needs physical space, enormous power, and political permission. All three are getting harder to secure.

Part ThreeThe AI Czar Exits — and xAI Cracks

On March 26th, David Sacks confirmed that his 130-day stint as the White House AI and crypto czar is over. He's moving to co-chair the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology (PCAST) alongside Michael Kratsios — a federal advisory body that studies issues and sends recommendations, but doesn't make policy. [9]

The PCAST lineup reads like a tech billionaire summit: Jensen Huang, Mark Zuckerberg, Larry Ellison, Sergey Brin, Marc Andreessen, Lisa Su, and Michael Dell. Sacks described it as the "most star power of any group like this" ever assembled. The council will take up AI, semiconductors, quantum computing, and nuclear power. [9]

What Sacks didn't address: whether his departure was accelerated by his recent comments on the "All In" podcast urging the administration to find an exit from the U.S. war with Iran. Trump responded by telling reporters that Sacks "hadn't spoken to him" about the war. [9]

Two days later, the other shoe dropped at xAI. Both of Elon Musk's last remaining co-founders — Manuel Kroiss (pretraining lead) and Ross Nordeen (operations lead, formerly involved in Twitter layoffs) — left the company. All 11 original xAI co-founders have now departed. Musk recently said xAI "was not built right the first time around" and is being "rebuilt from the foundations up" after being folded into SpaceX. [10]

The political and corporate leadership of AI is reshuffling. Sacks traded a direct line to Trump for an advisory seat full of billionaires. And the most provocative AI company in the industry just lost its entire founding team.

Part FourThe Money Keeps Moving

If the cracks are showing, nobody told the capital markets.

On March 31st, OpenAI closed $122 billion in funding — the largest private funding round in history — at an $852 billion valuation. [11] To fund its $30 billion share, SoftBank took on a $40 billion unsecured loan with a 12-month term from JPMorgan Chase, Goldman Sachs, and four Japanese banks — a bet widely seen as a signal that SoftBank's lenders believe the highly anticipated OpenAI IPO will happen by early 2027. SoftBank's total bet on OpenAI now exceeds $60 billion. [12]

The venture capital machine is running hot. Kleiner Perkins — the legendary firm behind early bets on Amazon and Google — raised $3.5 billion in fresh capital, up from $2 billion less than two years ago, with a lean team of just five partners. The firm holds early stakes in Together AI, Harvey, and Anthropic. [13] Kleiner joins a wave of mega-raises: Thrive Capital at $10 billion, General Catalyst targeting a similar amount, and Founders Fund closing $6 billion.

On the startup side, legal AI company Harvey confirmed an $11 billion valuation after a $200 million round co-led by GIC and Sequoia — the third Sequoia co-led round since Harvey's Series A. The company has now raised over $1 billion total, and its valuation jumped 3.5x in a year. [14]

And in Europe, Mistral AI raised $830 million in debt to build a data center near Paris powered by Nvidia chips, with plans to deploy 200 megawatts of compute capacity across Europe by 2027. Mistral has now raised over $3.1 billion in total. [15]

"Scaling our infrastructure in Europe is critical to empower our customers and to ensure AI innovation and autonomy remain at the heart of Europe."

Arthur Mensch, CEO of Mistral AI, March 30, 2026

The disconnect is growing. Public trust is collapsing. Congress is moving to constrain. And the money is flowing faster than ever. Something has to give.

Part FiveThe Trust Collapse

The people on the receiving end of the AI revolution are pushing back — and the data is brutal.

A Quinnipiac University poll published on March 30th found that 76% of Americans don't trust AI companies to do the right thing. 55% believe AI will do more harm than good to society. 70% expect AI to lead to significant job cuts. Among Gen Z — the generation most immersed in AI — that figure rises to 81%. Only 6% said they were "very excited" about AI. [16]

Institutions are responding. On March 26th, Wikipedia banned the use of AI-generated text by its editors. The policy, which passed 40–2, now explicitly states: "the use of LLMs to generate or rewrite article content is prohibited." Editors can still use AI to suggest basic copyedits to their own writing — but the encyclopedia that much of the internet feeds on has drawn a clear line. [17]

Spotify launched a beta "Artist Profile Protection" tool that lets musicians review and approve or decline releases before they appear on their profiles — a direct response to the tidal wave of AI-generated music being attributed to real artists. Sony Music has separately requested the removal of over 135,000 AI-generated songs impersonating its artists. [18]

Reddit rolled out new "human verification" requirements for accounts displaying suspicious behavior — another frontline response to the bot infestation that AI has accelerated. [19]

"When that happens, it can impact your catalog, your stats, your Release Radar, and how fans discover your music. One of the top requests we've heard from artists over the past year is that you want more visibility before music appears under your name."

Spotify, announcing Artist Profile Protection, March 24, 2026

The pattern is clear. The platforms that people actually use — Wikipedia, Spotify, Reddit — are all building walls against AI output. The companies building AI are raising billions. And three-quarters of the American public doesn't trust any of them. The gap between the builders and the built-for has never been wider.

Part SixOther Signals

Anthropic's Month From Hell

Anthropic shipped Claude Code v2.1.88 — and accidentally exposed ~2,000 source files containing roughly 512,000 lines of its own code. The leak set off a chain reaction: Anthropic filed a DMCA takedown that initially hit 8,100 GitHub repositories — including thousands of legitimate forks — before the company retracted and narrowed it to one repo and 96 forks. Separately, Anthropic acquired stealth biotech startup Coefficient Bio for $400 million in stock, and filed paperwork for AnthroPAC, a political action committee. [20]

OpenAI Kills Sora

OpenAI shut down Sora, its AI video generation tool, after it burned through roughly $1 million per day in compute costs while attracting fewer than 500,000 active users. The shutdown also blindsided Disney, which had been developing a $1 billion partnership around Sora. The closure came just months after launch and represents one of the industry's most expensive product failures. [21] [22]

OpenAI Executive Shuffle

OpenAI reshuffled its leadership. COO Brad Lightcap moved to a new "special projects" role reporting directly to Sam Altman. Fidji Simo is taking medical leave for a neuroimmune condition. Kate Rouch is stepping down to focus on cancer recovery. Kevin Weil (CPO), Chris Lehane, and Sarah Friar (CFO) remain. The changes come as OpenAI prepares for a potential IPO. [23]

ByteDance Enters the AI Video Vacuum

The same week OpenAI shut down Sora, ByteDance released Dreamina Seedance 2.0, its new AI video generation model, and made it available directly inside CapCut — the editing app already used by hundreds of millions. While OpenAI retreated from AI video, ByteDance walked right in. [24]

◆

The foundation is shifting.

The hardware is fracturing. The energy is running out. The politics are turning. The public doesn't trust it. And the money keeps moving anyway — $122 billion for OpenAI, $40 billion in debt for SoftBank, $3.5 billion for Kleiner Perkins. The AI industry is building faster than the world beneath it can hold. The cracks aren't fatal yet. But they're visible now.
